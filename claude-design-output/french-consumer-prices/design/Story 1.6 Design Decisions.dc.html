Pulse · Story 1.6 · design session record

French consumer prices: report design decisions

Decided against the published data, not a mock: 367 monthly headline observations (Jan 1996 – Jul 2026) and 343 category observations (Jan 1998 – Jul 2026) read out of the two Parquet publications. Every figure quoted below is a real published value. The companion design is Pulse CPI Report; the fixture declarations are in deliverables/visuals/.

1 · Standing questions and narrative hierarchy

The report is standing, not an article: it must answer the same questions every month, in the same order, whatever the numbers do. Four questions carry it, and they are also the four figures. Everything else was cut.

  1. Where do prices stand, and is the rate accelerating? Answered twice: in the scorecard as four figures, then as the trend figure. Both carry the same-month-a-year-ago comparison, because “2.1%” only means something beside “0.9% a year earlier”.
  2. What is carrying the annual rate? INSEE’s four published contributions, stacked against the headline rate. Rents are answered here too, in their own lane and on their own scale.
  3. Which components diverge from headline, and how much of the basket are they? Small multiples, each panel carrying its basket share so a 12.6% energy move is read against a 7.6% basket share.
  4. What has the price level done cumulatively? Index levels since Base 2025 = 100 — the answer the annual rate hides when it falls while levels keep climbing.

Hierarchy: answer first, evidence second. A reader who stops after the scorecard has the month’s answer in four numbers and one sentence; each figure below then earns its place by answering a question the ones above it cannot.

2 · Indicators, precision, comparisons, periods

Every value is displayed at the precision the provider publishes. No rounding up for tidiness, no derived decimals: the contracts declare DECIMAL(12,2) for levels and DECIMAL(8,1) for changes, and the report shows exactly that.

Represented periods

Default five years (Aug 2021 – Jul 2026): long enough to hold the 2022–23 episode and the 2026 turn, short enough that a monthly bar stays legible. Also 24 months and “since 2015”. Full history back to 1996 is deliberately not offered in the standing view: at 367 monthly bars the composition figure becomes texture.

Comparisons

Three, and only three: against the same month a year earlier (scorecard and lead), against the headline rate (every component panel and the contribution figure), and against the 2025 base (level figure). No forecasts, no targets, no cross-country comparison — that needs another source.

Rounding rule

Charts and prose show one decimal for percentages and contributions, two for levels. The Pulse rent contribution is published at three decimals; figures show two, and the accessible table carries all three. Signs are explicit (+2.1%, −0.3%) so a fall is never a bare number.

One structural decision worth flagging. Axis labels, tick labels, the selected-point readout and the end-of-line values are real DOM text positioned over the plot, not <text> inside the SVG. The SVG carries geometry only. That keeps every label at its true font size under 400% zoom and text-only scaling instead of shrinking with the viewBox, and keeps it selectable. Visuals therefore receive display.width in CSS pixels from the report and draw at 1 unit = 1 px; they never measure the DOM themselves.

5 · Exploration controls and parameterized queries

The report opts into exploration while its default composition stays fully pre-composed: on load, with no interaction, it already answers all four questions for the latest month. Three controls, each bound to a query parameter, plus a download of exactly what is on screen.

Where each concern lives

SQL, dataset selection, exploration state, provenance strings and routing all sit in the report module. It runs the parameterized queries, validates the result against the visual’s declared schema, maps columns to the visual’s field names, and passes (rows, display, provenance). A visual receives arrays and numbers, returns an element, and declares cleanup. The month control changes no query at all — it is a selection index over rows already in hand, so scrubbing it never touches the database.

6 · Provenance and accessible data equivalents

Two levels, no third

A header block carries source, licence, dataset names, contract version and the latest published month — visible without interaction. Each figure then carries a disclosure with its own provider series identifiers, precision, query and data table. Per-figure footer lines were rejected: four repetitions of the same attribution is noise, and the identifiers differ per figure anyway.

Provider vs Pulse

Anything Pulse computes is labelled where it is read, not only in a footnote: the rent lane says “Pulse calculation, separate scale” in its legend, the rent columns say “(Pulse)”, and the method section states the formula. The rent contribution is never stacked with the four official ones, because rents already sit inside services.

Data equivalents

Every figure ships a table of the same rows it drew, in the same disclosure as its query, capped to a scroll region so it never buries the page. Each chart also carries a written aria-label naming the series, the window, the range and the latest value — a sentence, not “chart”. The prose read-out under each figure states the answer in words, so the finding survives with images off.

7 · State treatments

Failures are scoped to whoever owns them. A query belongs to the report, so a query failure replaces the figures wholesale rather than showing four broken panels. Schema and render failures belong to one visual, so they stay inside that figure and the other three keep answering.
